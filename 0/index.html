[Draft] The Optimal Geometry of Contrastive Learning Objectives

Chungpa Lee · Updated Oct 23, 2026

Contrastive learning uses relationships between inputs to learn vector representations, called embeddings. Its objectives encourage related inputs to have similar embeddings and other pairs to remain separated.[4][5]

The loss function, the pairs included in each batch, and the use of class labels can favor different arrangements. Studying the embeddings that minimize each objective makes these geometric differences explicit.[1][2][3]

01

Contrastive learning

An encoder maps an input to a vector, or list of numbers, called an embedding. Treating embeddings as points in a space makes their similarities visible.[4][5]

Data inputsE.g. image, text
→
EncoderE.g. ResNet, ViT
→
EmbeddingsNumerical vectors

A positive pair consists of two inputs defined as related, or their embeddings. A negative pair is treated as a non-match under the chosen rule.

Cropping or rotating an image produces another version, or view, of the same image. These two views can form a positive pair. An image with its caption, or two examples sharing a class label, provide other ways to define positives.[4][5]

Four instances, eight embeddings

Consider four original examples, called instances, with two related views of each. With n=4 instances, there are eight embeddings: u₁ and v₁ correspond to instance 1, and similarly for the others. A shared index identifies a matching pair.

Normalize every vector to length 1, placing the points on a unit sphere. Similarity then depends on direction: 1 means the same point, 0 means a right angle, and −1 means opposite directions.

For this example, only views of the same instance are positive. The matrix lists every u–v comparison: four positive entries on the diagonal and twelve negative entries elsewhere. The two numbers beside the matrix average these two groups.[2]

What does the loss minimize?

The two goals are to give positive pairs high similarity and negative pairs low similarity. A loss function turns these preferences into a numerical penalty. Training changes the encoder to reduce this number.

To study the geometry favored by the loss, optimize the unit embeddings directly. Which arrangement attains the smallest possible loss? Such an arrangement is a global optimum of this embedding problem.

The analysis assumes sufficient dimension for the configurations below. It characterizes the objective’s preference; a particular encoder may impose additional restrictions.

02

Softmax vs. sigmoid contrastive losses

InfoNCE is a softmax contrastive loss that identifies the positive match among competing candidates.[7] Pairwise sigmoid loss classifies each pair as positive or negative.[6]

Consider n instances, each represented by two unit vectors uᵢ and vᵢ in a d-dimensional embedding space. Pairs with the same index are positive, other u–v pairs are negative, and similarity is the inner product.

The analysis minimizes the loss directly over these embeddings in sufficient dimension. The figures in this section use n=3.[1]

With u₁ as the reference, or anchor, the candidate embeddings are v₁, v₂, and v₃. Only v₁ is its positive match.

InfoNCE applies softmax to similarities divided by a temperature parameter τ. For u₁, it divides exp(u₁ᵀv₁ / τ) by the sum of all three exponentiated scores. This is the probability assigned to the positive match; the loss is its negative logarithm. The symmetric form used here averages both directions, giving six anchor terms in total.[4]

Sigmoid assigns a separate penalty to each matrix entry: three for positive pairs and six for negative pairs. Temperature τ controls the sensitivity to similarity differences; smaller τ amplifies a given difference in similarity.[1][6]

Each sigmoid term depends on one pair, so separate blocks can be evaluated independently and summed. Each InfoNCE term requires its full row or column denominator. InfoNCE can also be evaluated in blocks, but normalizing each block independently changes the objective.[6]

Why the two goals create a tradeoff

Because pairs share embeddings, their similarities cannot be chosen independently. In this three-instance example, setting every negative u–v similarity to −1 forces all u vectors to coincide and all v vectors to lie at the opposite point. Every positive similarity is then also −1.

Keeping positive similarity at 1 makes each matching pair coincide, but leaves their spacing free. If all points coincide, the negative mean is also 1. Spreading the instance directions lowers this mean, down to −1/(n−1).

An evenly spaced arrangement called a simplex ETF reaches this lower bound. It is an equilateral triangle for three instances, or a tetrahedron for four. The right-hand figure shows how negative spacing can change while positives stay aligned.[1][2]

CCEM reduces the search to one similarity

The paper proposes the double-Constant Embedding Model (CCEM) to reduce optimization over all embeddings to a single parameter. It describes the smallest attainable mean negative similarity at each fixed mean positive similarity.

In CCEM, all positive u–v pairs share one similarity and all negative u–v pairs share another. Under the conditions below, the paper proves that this one-parameter family contains a global minimizer of sigmoid loss.

With three instances, each view forms an equilateral triangle. Moving the triangle centers apart shrinks the triangles so every vector stays on the sphere. Both positive and negative similarities decrease.

If the views represent images and text, the distance between these centers is a modality gap: the two types of input have different average embeddings.

For three instances, the relationship below gives the negative similarity once the positive similarity is fixed. The slider shows the corresponding arrangement.[1]

For unit embeddings with d≥n, τ>0, and fixed b≥0, CCEM contains a global minimizer of sigmoid loss (Corollary 2). Under these conditions, minimizing the CCEM loss curve yields a globally optimal configuration of the full embedding problem.[1]

Temperature selects the optimal structure

Along CCEM, increasing positive similarity also increases the difference between positive and negative similarity. InfoNCE therefore decreases until the views align in a simplex ETF. Temperature changes the curve’s scale and slope, while its minimum remains at positive similarity 1.

For sigmoid, the gain from lowering negative similarities can outweigh the cost of misaligning positives. Its minimum can occur before full alignment, leaving the two view centers apart. In the fixed-bias example below, increasing τ closes this gap; full alignment begins at τ≈0.185.[1]

The bias is fixed at b=1 here. For sigmoid loss, the paper uses the coefficient t in place of 1/τ. Its Theorem 2 treats the separate setting t=b.[1]

03

Full-batch vs. mini-batch training

Training often processes examples in small groups called mini-batches. When the loss includes only comparisons within each group, the omitted pairs can leave the overall geometry partly undetermined.[2]

Two batches can move independently

Consider four instances, each represented by two unit embeddings uᵢ and vᵢ. InfoNCE scores each positive match relative to the candidate matches in its batch.[7] Full-batch InfoNCE compares all four instances together. A fixed partition into batches {1,2} and {3,4} gives each batch its own comparisons and denominator. Keeping this partition fixed isolates the effect of omitted comparisons.

The full-batch loss includes all twelve negative u–v pairs; the fixed mini-batch losses include four. During the rotation stage, uncheck Reveal all pairs to mark the other eight as N/A (“not compared”).

Each two-instance batch minimizes its loss by aligning positive pairs and placing its two instances at opposite ends of a line. Rotating either line preserves all similarities within its batch. In the first stage of the slider, the mini-batch loss remains at its minimum while similarities between batches change.

The second stage deforms the four instance directions from perpendicular lines into an evenly spaced tetrahedron (simplex ETF when n=4). Within-batch negative similarities increase from −1 to −1/3, while between-batch similarities decrease from 0 to −1/3. This attains the full-batch minimum while increasing the fixed mini-batch loss.

To compare these configurations, the statistics below include all u–v pairs, including those omitted from the mini-batch loss.[2]

Expectation = mean

Variance = mean squared distance from the mean

Same mean, different geometry

Every arrangement on this path has mean negative similarity −1/3. Some configurations contain both coincident and strongly separated negative pairs; the tetrahedron assigns every negative pair the same similarity. The mean alone does not distinguish these geometries.

The variance of negative-pair similarities falls from 8/9 to 2/9 during rotation, then reaches 0 at the tetrahedron. Positive pairs remain aligned throughout, so their mean is 1 and variance is 0.

For fixed disjoint batches, the upper and lower bounds on the variance of negative similarities at global optima decrease with batch size (Theorem 5.5). Both bounds reach zero for the full batch.[2]

The full-batch optimum also suggests an additional training penalty: penalize squared deviations of negative similarities from −1/(n−1), with n counting all instances. In experiments with randomly sampled batches, this reduces negative-similarity variance. The effect on task performance varies across experiments.[2]

04

Self-supervised vs. supervised losses

Views of different instances in the same class are negative pairs for the self-supervised loss and positive pairs for the supervised loss. Their relative weights determine whether the optimum preserves within-class variation or exhibits class collapse, where all instances of a class share one embedding.[3]

Instance matches and class matches

Consider four instances, each represented by two unit embeddings uᵢ and vᵢ. Instances 1–2 belong to Class A and 3–4 to Class B. The pair u₁–v₁ matches two views of one instance. The pair u₁–v₂ matches different instances only because they share Class A.[3]

The objective analyzed here separates the two sources of attraction. ℒself matches views of the same instance. ℒsup adds matches between different instances in the same class.

In this decomposition, u₁–v₁ is a positive in ℒself. It appears only in the denominator of ℒsup; its same-instance attraction is supplied by ℒself. Eqs. (2)–(3).

ℒα = (1 − α)ℒsup + αℒself

For u₁–v₂, the two terms compete: ℒself encourages separation, while ℒsup encourages similarity. The weight α controls their relative strength. Increasing α favors instance separation. If class attraction dominates, the instances of a class can collapse to one point, eliminating their differences in the embedding space.[3]

SSEM reduces the search to within-class spread

The paper proposes the Simplex-to-Simplex Embedding Model (SSEM) to characterize the embeddings that minimize the combined loss as α changes. Each instance’s two views remain aligned. Different instances within a class share one similarity, while instances from different classes share another.

At one endpoint, each class has collapsed and the two class centers sit at opposite poles. At the other, all four instances occupy the vertices of a tetrahedron (simplex ETF when n=4). The name refers to these endpoints: a simplex of class centers and a simplex of individual instances.

A single parameter controls the spread around the class centers and the separation between classes. The shared pair similarities determine the loss, reducing the search over all embeddings to one parameter. In the four-instance figure, within-class variance measures this spread: zero means collapse, and 2/3 gives the tetrahedron.[3]

For this combined loss, balanced classes, and unit embeddings in sufficient dimension (d≥3 here), SSEM contains a global minimizer. In this example, it is sufficient to vary within-class variance from 0 to 2/3. The minimum of this curve identifies a globally optimal embedding configuration.

As α increases from 0 with temperature τ fixed at 0.5, the minimizing within-class variance remains zero up to α≈0.518. Above this threshold, the optimum has positive variance and retains variation within each class. At α=1, the optimal configuration is the tetrahedron.[3]

What the geometry reveals

An optimal embedding configuration can exhibit a modality gap, unequal negative similarities, or class collapse. Different measurements distinguish these geometric properties.

  • Positive similarity shows whether matching views align.
  • Negative mean and variance describe average separation and differences hidden by that average.
  • Within-class variance shows how much variation remains inside each class.
